What the financial ratios tell us about industries

This notebook asks how to handle outliers and missing values in three financial ratios, and whether those ratios really differ between industries. No model is trained.

Notebook sections 2 and 3

What is wrong with the data?

"Bad data" is several different problems, so each kind is counted separately.

  • Missing: EBITDA is the real gap, at 62%.
  • inf: a division by zero, so revenue should be zero. The sign only says whether profit was positive or negative. -inf is ten times more common than +inf for net margin, so most of these companies have no revenue and make a loss.
  • Negative margins are normal: 7,521 companies simply lose money.
  • Negative asset turnover is unusual: the notebook calls these 117 values data errors, but the check further down suggests most are real negative revenue.
  • The median looks normal, the min and max do not. That already shows that extreme values exist.

Does inf really mean zero revenue?

This is a test of the idea. If revenue is 0, then asset_turnover = revenue / assets must also be 0.

The saved outputs do not show what the 64 exceptions are. Asset turnover being missing would be one explanation, since missing also counts as "not 0".

Are the 117 negative turnovers just flipped signs?

This check is not in the notebook. If a minus sign were a typo in this one column, two things should hold: the size of the value should look normal for its industry, and the company's margins should look like everyone else's. A negative turnover needs negative revenue or negative assets. Negative revenue would also flip the sign of a loss-making company's margin, so a loss would appear as a positive margin.

Result: they are not simple sign typos. Their size does not match their industries (p = 0.000002 against a typo), with 33% in the lowest tenth. Their margins are also very unusual: 97% are positive, with a median of 1.235. This pattern fits negative revenue together with a loss. 65% are in Financial Services, where investment losses booked as revenue would be one explanation (hypothesis). Taking the absolute value would be wrong, so keeping them as missing is still the right rule. The ratios alone cannot prove this, because there is no revenue column.

Cleaning rules

The flag uses OR. A company with zero revenue and zero profit gives 0/0 = NaN, not inf, so the margin alone would miss it. Asset turnover of 0 still catches it. In total 2,005 companies are flagged (1,938 + 64 + 3 from the table above).

Notebook section 4

Is the missing data random?

If values were missing at random, filling them would be harmless. If missingness depends on the industry, then being missing is itself a signal.

  • Chi-square test of independence. Build a table of industry × flag. Compute how many flagged companies each industry would have if the flag had nothing to do with industry, and add up how far reality is from that.
  • Cramér's V. With 27,000 rows, tiny differences get tiny p-values, so p only shows that an effect exists. V rescales chi-square to 0-1, where 0.1 is small, 0.3 medium and 0.5 large.

Cramér's V: how strongly each flag depends on industry

Result: missingness is not random. All three p-values show as 0.0e+00, which is a display effect, so report them as p < 0.001. The useful finding is the ranking in V: whether a company has a financial row barely depends on industry (chi² 187), EBITDA missing does more (1,378), and zero revenue depends on it strongly (2,855).

Hypothesis: banks at 68% fits, since EBITDA is not a meaningful number for a bank.

What does not fit that story: Insurance is at only 41%, and Semiconductors and Technology Hardware are the highest with almost no zero-revenue companies. This may reflect how the data was collected, but this notebook cannot show that.

Which way of filling gaps is best?

A model needs a number in every cell, so the notebook rules out options first.

The experiment hides a random 20% of known values, learns both medians on the other 80% (countries with fewer than 10 values use the global median, as in Daniel's notebook), fills the hidden values, and measures the mean absolute error in signed-log units. Lower is better.

Result: the country median always wins, but on margins by less than 1%. Only asset turnover gains visibly. Both errors stay large, so neither rule recovers the real value well. This is why the simple global median is defensible, and it is what ExtraFeatures in notebook 02 uses. Country reaches the model through its own one-hot columns anyway.

The missing flag. Filling gives thousands of companies the identical invented value, which looks more certain than it is. The flag tells the model the number was invented. The flag columns are built in ExtraFeatures, not in this notebook.

Notebook section 5

How bad are the outliers?

  • Compare mean and median. A typical net margin is 3.8%, but the mean says 180,000%. The mean is useless here.
  • Tukey fence: Q1 - 3 × IQR to Q3 + 3 × IQR. IQR is the width of the middle 50%.
  • Why not mean ± 3 std? Here that would mean a fence of about ±716,000 on net margin, so almost nothing would count as an outlier. The outliers would hide themselves. Quartiles barely move, whatever the extremes do.
  • 15% is far more than a few bad rows. Deleting everything beyond the fence would lose about one in seven companies with a net margin. The distribution is heavy-tailed, and that is a strong argument against deleting.

Are the extremes errors or real?

Revenue is at the bottom of a margin but at the top of asset turnover. So if tiny revenue causes the giant margin, asset turnover should be close to 0 in the same row.

The 5 highest and 5 lowest net margins

Result: 9 of 10 have asset turnover within 0.0011 of zero, which supports "tiny revenue, ratio blows up". One is low but less extreme. The notebook shows NaN for the two largest, because their turnover is a tiny negative number and cleaning removed it. The industries also fit the zero-revenue list. So "real but unhelpful" is the best reading, not a proof.

The treatment: shrink, do not delete

  1. Signed log: sign(v) * log(1 + |v|). A plain log fails on negative margins and on 0. Normal values barely change, giant values collapse, and the order stays the same.
  2. Clipping at the 1st and 99th percentiles. Values beyond the limits are set to the limit, and the company stays in the data.

Do financial distributions differ between industries?

This section uses medians because they are stable, and _clean values because filled-in medians would fake similarity.

Show as a table

    The smallest industries are Consumer Staples Retail (178 companies) and Household Products (226), so their medians are the least reliable. The largest are Materials (3,917) and Capital Goods (3,125).

    Is the difference statistically significant?

    • Kruskal-Wallis test. Could all 25 industries come from the same distribution? Rank all companies from lowest to highest, ignoring industry. If industry did not matter, every industry would have about the same average rank. H grows the more those average ranks differ.
    • Why not ANOVA? ANOVA compares means and assumes roughly normal data, and neither is true here. Ranks do not care if the largest value is 10 or 35 million.
    • Effect size guide: 0.01 small, 0.06 medium, 0.14 large.

    Effect size: how much of the rank variation industry explains

    Result: asset turnover separates industries best. All three ratios differ by industry, with p-values too small to display (report p < 0.001). Industry explains about 25% of the variation in asset turnover ranks. That makes sense, because asset turnover describes the business model, while margins also depend on how a company is doing right now (hypothesis).
    Notebook section 6, then notebook 02

    Can financials separate the pairs the classifier confuses?

    Kruskal-Wallis only says that some industries differ. For classification, what matters is whether the ones we mix up differ. Each of 11 pairs is tested on each ratio, which gives 33 tests.

    • Mann-Whitney U is the two-group version of Kruskal-Wallis.
    • Bonferroni correction: with 33 tests, some would look significant by luck, so each p is multiplied by 33.
    • AUC = U / (n1 × n2): the probability that a random company from the first industry has a higher value than one from the second. 0.5 means no separation. 0.30 is as strong as 0.70, just in the other direction.

    21 of 33 comparisons are significant after correction. Significant does not mean useful, so the chart shows how far each AUC is from 0.5.
